The Flixborough explosion, UK 1974

❑ The failure of a bypass assembly introduced into a train of six cyclohexane oxidation reactors after one of the reactors was removed owing to the development of a leak.
❑ The leaking reactor was constructed of 12.3 mm mild steel plate with 3 mm stainless steel bonded to it
❑ It developed a vertical crack in the mild steel outer layer of the reactor from which cyclohexane leaked leading to the removal of the reactor.
❑ One of the factors that contributed to the crack was stress corrosion, resulting from the presence of nitrates that had contaminated river water being used to cool a leaking flange
❑ Mild steel exposed to hot nitrate solution and to stresses which in extreme cases may be well below the yield strength, and may remain in the structure from the fabrication procedure or may derive from operating stresses, especially if intensified by a defect, can develop intergranular cracks. Such stress corrosion cracking results from the conjoint action of stress, of an appropriate magnitude, and a corrosive environment, of specific composition, upon steel having a wide range of compositions or structures

UNIFORM CORROSION

❖ Uniform corrosion can be measured in the laboratory

❑ This form of corrosion involves a uniform regular removal of metal from the surface
❑ Uniform corrosion of metals is the simplest and most frequently observed form of corrosion, being found when items are exposed to the atmosphere, the soil, and an enormous variety of a aqueous solutions.
❑ Uniform corrosion normally affects metallic surfaces that have a uniform chemical composition and a microstructure with homogeneous access to the corrosive solution, which itself needs to be uniform.
❑ The corroded surface may become covered with the corrosion products or completely clean, depending on many factors.
❑ The uniform corrosion rate normally increases with temperature.
❑ Concentration of the corrosive chemical in the solution has a strong effect on corrosion rate.

--- Page 30 ---

Measures to control uniform Corrosion

* Selection of materials that can are corrosion resistant, which means the corrosion rate is small enough for the application. The resistance of these materials to corrosion usually is a result of passivation.
* Introduction of a barrier between the metal and the corrosive environment – Surface modification the use coatings and surface treatments .
  * For example : Painting is an organic barrier; Chrome plating, an inorganic barrier.
* When adopting this type of strategy for corrosion control, always consider that coatings are not perfect i.e the coatings could have pinholes that can affect its performance
  * E.g. A defect on a chrome coating creates an anodic spot that suffers a high corrosion rate, because the ratio between anodic to cathodic areas results in a high density of anodic current.
  * The same defect on a galvanized plate does not create an anodic spot, because zinc is anodic relative to steel, and the region below the damaged coating is protected from corrosion.

Reference: Luiz Otávio Amaral Affonso, in Machinery Failure Analysis Handbook, 2006

Measures to control uniform Corrosion (Continued)

* Modifications of the corrosive medium via introduction of corrosion inhibitors
  * Corrosion inhibitors may reduce the corrosion rate to an acceptable level. This strategy is commonly used in the processing industry.
* Cathodic protection: This involves forcing the metal to assume an electric potential relative to the corrosive medium that will not allow corrosion to take place. This is often done in buried pipelines with the introduction of an external power source to change the aforementioned potential
* Anodic protection - the introduction of anodes in contact with the main structure and the corrosive medium. This often is done to protect oceangoing ships and other structures. A zinc or aluminum anode is attached to the ship hull and consumed instead of the steel

Reference: Luiz Otávio Amaral Affonso, in Machinery Failure Analysis Handbook, 2006

PITTING CORROSION

• Pitting corrosion, also known as pitting, is a highly localized and aggressive form of corrosion that occurs on metal surfaces
• Small areas of the metal corrode preferentially, forming pits, while the rest of the surface remains virtually free from attack.
• Visually, pitting is characterized by one or several small cavities or holes on the surface of the metal. Pits can come in several shapes and sizes; they can be deep and narrow, shallow and wide, or undercut beneath the surface.

❑ Pitting corrosion is a localized form of corrosion

❑ Pitting corrosion is more destructive than uniform corrosion is because it is difficult to predict as corrosion products may cover the pits,

❑ It is also difficult to design against the formation of pits, as well as to predict when this type of corrosion may occur

❑ Passive metals are much more prone to pitting corrosion than non-passive metals

❑ The naturally occurring passive films formed over many metallic substrates (such as aluminum, copper, stainless steel, etc.) can provide a considerable corrosion resistance over a long time in corrosive media. However, if corrosion occurs (due to material defects or changes in the surrounding environment), some pits could form.

❑ Pitting is most likely to occur in a halide ion containing media (mostly chloride) in the presence of oxygen or oxidizing agents. Stainless steels are very prone to pitting in chloride containing environments

CREVICE CORROSION

❑ Crevice corrosion is a type of localized corrosion, that occurs at narrow spaces (gaps/crevices) formed by two joining surfaces.

❑ The crevice can be formed between two metals or between a metal and non-metal material (e.g. metal/gasket interface)

❑ Outside the Crevice or without the crevice, both metals are resistant to corrosion i.e it is the gap between the two surfaces that facilitate the corrosion

❑ A crevice shields part of the surface and thereby enhances the formation of differential aeration cells and chloride concentration cells

❑ Crevice corrosion is initiated by a difference in concentration of some chemical constituents, usually oxygen in the crevice. This concentration difference facilitate the formation of an electrochemical concentration cell (differential aeration cell in the case of oxygen).

--- Page 42 ---

❑ Usually, the electrolyte in the crevice (gap) is in a stagnant state (see the diagram) and the oxygen concentration in the crevice is much lower than its concentration outside the crevice (gap). This impairs passivity making the crevice an initiation site for metal dissolution (corrosion)

❑ As a result, the metal surface in the crevice (gap) acts as an anode, and the adjacent outer metal surface plays the role of a cathode ( see the diagram) If the crevice attack begins, it progresses very quickly.

[DIAGRAM: "Steel" forms the bottom metal layer with a pit under a "Shielding object"; "O2 from air" is at the top with a downward arrow entering the electrolyte; "O2" with a downward arrow to "OH-" is located left of the "Shielding object"; "Na+" and "Cl-" are in the electrolyte to the left; another "Na+" is near the "Shielding object"; "Cl-" has a curved arrow entering the pit under the "Shielding object"; "Fe2+" has a vertical arrow pointing up from "Steel" inside the pit; "H+" and "Cl-" are inside the pit; a dashed arrow labeled "e-" points from inside the pit to the outer metal surface near "OH-"]

❑ Crevice corrosion can also result from retention of water or corrosive solution within a crevice, while the outer surface is dried or exposed only to a relatively non-corrosive environment

❑ A corrosive environment may be formed by deposits or wet packing materials

❑ Example : thermal insulation coverings used to maintain temperature in piping and containment vessels. Themal cycling leads to condensation, evaporation and concentration of solutes leads to general corrosion of carbonsteel and in some cases SCC of stainless steel under insulation

Prevention

❑ Generally, environmental conditions such as pH, oxygen concentration, chloride concentration and temperature also play major role in initiation and propagation of crevice corrosion.
❑ As a rule, corrosion is more intense in chloride environments.

All passive metal/metal alloys whose corrosion resistance depend on the stability of passive films are particularly susceptible to crevice corrosion. Examples are all grades of stainless steels and aluminium alloys.

Crevice corrosion can be designed out of the system:
• Use welded butt joints instead of riveted or bolted joints in new equipment
• Eliminate crevices in existing lap joints by continuous welding or soldering
• Avoid creating stagnant conditions and ensure complete drainage in vessels
• Use solid, non-absorbent gaskets such as Teflon.
• Use higher alloys for increased resistance to crevice corrosion

Intergranular Corrosion, IGC

❑ IGC is an attack that occurs at the grain boundaries of a metal or alloy due to the precipitation of a secondary phase.
❑ IGC occurs when the metal on or adjacent to the grain boundaries dissolves, or corrodes preferentially to the grain interiors.
❑ The grain boundary region acts as an anode with the grain interior being a cathode, forming a galvanic cell with a large cathode and a small anode in which rapid corrosion occurs at the anode

❑ Intergranular corrosion (IGC) can seriously weaken the bonding force between crystal grains and the mechanical strength of metals and alloys would be markedly damaged due to IGC. The combination of stress and IGC, for some alloys/environment systems will cause intergranular stress corrosion cracking (IGSCC) (Zhou and Zuo, 2015).

EXAMPLES OF Intergranular Corrosion, IGC
1. In aluminum-copper alloys, precipitation of Al2Cu particles at the grain boundaries leaves the adjacent solid solution (i.e the grains) anodic and more prone to corrosion.
2. In austenitic stainless steel precipitation of chromium carbides at the grain boundaries results in IGC

Intergranular Corrosion, IGC

❑ IGC is an attack that occurs at the grain boundaries of a metal or alloy due to the precipitation of a secondary phase.

❑ Corrosion activity may develop because of some heterogeneity in the grain boundary structure

For Example:.
1. In aluminium-copper alloys, precipitation of Al2Cu particles at the grain boundaries leaves the adjacent solid solution (i.e the grains) anodic and more prone to corrosion.
2. In austenitic stainless steel precipitation of chromium carbides at the grain boundaries results in IGC

❑ Intergranular corrosion is caused by a difference in the electrochemical potentials between the bulk of the grain and the grain boundaries where intermetallic phases precipitate

Intergranular corrosion of Austenitic Stainless steel - Sensitization

❑ Austenitic stainless steels are non-magnetic stainless steels that contain high levels of chromium and nickel and low levels of carbon.

❑ Austenitic SS are the 300 series e.g. 304SS, 316SS, 904 SS

❑ Austenitic SS when heated to between 425oC to 800oC, insoluble chromium carbides precipitate at the grain boundaries

❑ The Cr depleted region in the GB then becomes less corrosion resistance than the surrounding grains

❑ The chromium depleted zone becomes the preferential path for corrosion attack or crack propagation if under tensile stress

❑ < 425oC ..carbon diffusion is low so that carbides cannot be formed but above 815oC The chromium carbide formed is soluble

PREVENTION OF IGC in AUSTENITIC STAINLESS STEEL

❑ Use low carbon (e.g. 304L, 316L) grade of stainless steels
❑ Use stabilized grades alloyed with titanium (for example type 321) or niobium (for example type 347). Titanium and niobium are strong carbide-formers. They react with the carbon to form the corresponding carbides thereby preventing chromium depletion.
❑ Use post-weld heat treatment.

ENVIRONMENTALLY INDUCED CRACKING

❑ Failure of a metal alloy in the presence of an environment that causes minimal uniform corrosion
❑ It is the brittle failure of a ductile material due to the combined effect of corrosion and tensile stress
❑ Environmentally induced cracking occurs very rapidly and can be catastrophic

STRESS CORROSION CRACKING

• Stress corrosion cracking (SCC) is defined as the growth of cracks due to the simultaneous action of a stress (nominally static and tensile) and a reactive environment.
• SCC is the result of the combined and synergistic interactions of mechanical stress and corrosion/oxidation reactions.

Stress corrosion cracking occurs when a susceptible material is subjected to an environment that causes stress corrosion cracking and the application of a tensile stress.

STRESS CORROSION CRACKING

❑ The tensile stresses may be in the form of directly applied stresses or in the form of residual stress
❑ Operation such as Cold deformation and forming, welding, heat treatment, machining and grinding can introduce residual stresses
❑ Usually, the fine cracks penetrate into the material while most of the surface of the component remains unattacked,
❑ SCC is characterized by fine cracks which lead to failure of components
❑ Failures due to SCC are more often sudden and unpredictable which may occur after as little as few months or years of previously satisfactory service

      ❖ Residual stresses in fabricated components can be removed by stress relief annealing
      ❖ Introduce surface compressive stresses e.g. shoot peening or grit blasting (esp. wedded surfaces)

3) Remove critical environmental species such as hydroxides, chlorides, and oxygen.

4) Avoid stagnant areas and crevices in heat exchangers where chloride and hydroxide might become concentrated.

CORROSION FATIGUE CRACKING

❑ Corrosion fatigue is ‘the process in which a metal fractures prematurely under conditions of simultaneous corrosion and repeated cyclic loading at lower stress levels or fewer cycles than would be required in the absence of the corrosive environment.’ (ASTM standard definition ASTM, 1986, p. 180),

❑ The fatigue process is thought to cause rupture of the protective passive film for passive metals, upon which corrosion is accelerated. If the metal is simultaneously exposed to a corrosive environment, the failure can take place at even lower loads and after shorter time.

❑ Corrosion fatigue crack usually forms more slowly and corrosion products are more likely to be present in the crack

❑ The fatigue fracture is brittle and the cracks are most often transgranular, as in stress-corrosion cracking, but not branched.

--- Page 62 ---

❑ Reduce stress concentration or redistribute stress (balance strength and stress throughout the component)
   ✔ Stress raisers such as notches of surface roughness increases susceptibility to corrosion fatigue

❑ Select the correct shape of critical sections

❑ Provide against rapid changes of loading, temperature or pressure

❑ Avoid internal stress

❑ Limit corrosion factor in the corrosion-fatigue process (more resistant material / less corrosive environment).

# HYDROGEN INDUCED CRACKING

* Hydrogen-induced cracking (HIC) is generally a common problem encountered in steel exposed to hydrogen rich environments such as hydrogen sulfide environments found in sour oil and gas wells and some power plants
* High strength structural steels which are welded are exposed to a certain hydrogen pick-up which means a risk at fracture and blistering due to HIC.
  * During welding, hydrogen is absorbed by the weld pool from the arc atmosphere.
  * During cooling, much of this hydrogen escapes from the solidified bead by diffusion but some also diffuses into the HAZ and the parent metal.
  * The amount of hydrogen gas which does so depends on several factors such as the original amount absorbed, the size of the weld, the decreasing solubility, and the time-temperature conditions of cooling.
* Hydrogen sulfide, H2S acts as a catalyst for hydrogen ingress in the steels.
* Hydrogen-induced cracking can occur on a very wide variety of steel equipment: drill strings, underground hydrocarbon extraction tubes (Oil Country Tubular Goods), pipelines, casing maintenance pieces,s

--- Page 64 ---

**HIC occurs without external application of stress**

* The presence of hydrogen atoms in a metal degrades some of its mechanical properties, especially its ductility, leading in some cases to embrittlement.
* Additionally, hydrogen atoms diffuse through metals and coalesce to form hydrogen molecules at certain preferred locations such as inclusions.
* Hydrogen atoms are soluble in ferrite (which is the major phase of most steels). At discrete inclusions in the steel (e.g. manganese sulphide, which is present in many steels)
* The hydrogen atoms combine to form hydrogen molecules.
* Hydrogen molecules are insoluble in the steel lattice and these molecules associate, producing high tensile stresses which can initiate hydrogen cracking.
* When laminations are present in the steel, hydrogen molecules form at the interface between them, creating blisters.

--- Page 65 ---

* At high temperatures hydrogen atoms can react with carbides in the steel producing methane gas.
* Methane is insoluble in the steel structure and causes blistering or cracking. In clean steels containing none of these features the hydrogen atoms pass straight through the steel, causing no damage.
* The main factor that promotes the entry of hydrogen into a metal is the presence on the metal of a surface poison such as sulphide or other species which inhibit the hydrogen recombination reaction.
* HIC develops without applied stresses. Cracking results both from the stresses induced by the internal pressure due to the recombination of hydrogen on specific sites in the microstructure, and from the hydrogen embrittling effect, weakening the cohesive forces within the metal.

DEALLOYING

GRAPHITIC CORROSION

❑ Graphitic corrosion occurs exclusively in gray cast iron
❑ Graphitic corrosion refers to the deterioration of grey cast iron pipes, in which the metallic constituents are leached or turned into corrosion products, leaving the graphite intact and exposed.
❖ Gray cast iron has a continuous graphite network in its microstructure
❖ The graphite acts as the cathode and accelerates anodic dissolution of nearby iron, leaving behind the graphite network which is brittle

❑ What Causes Graphitic Corrosion?

Graphitic corrosion is caused by prolonged exposure of gray cast iron to minerals and compounds found in soils and in water. It is commonly found in cast Fe pipes that are exposed to salt water, acidic mine water, dilute acids, and soils; especially those that contain chlorides from deicing salt or sulphate reducing bacteria.

--- Page 72 ---

❑ Graphitic corrosion is often found in cast iron piping used for water mains and sewage systems. Such piping is usually buried in damp soil where the corrosion is unnoticed until there is a catastrophic leak.

❑ Graphitic corrosion is also common in fire sprinkler valves, steam system heat exchangers, and marine exhaust systems made of cast fe.

The presence of graphitic corrosion can be difficult to determine before a failure. Rust bleeding from the part, corrosion that extends beneath the normal surface corrosion present on cast iron, and a loss of attraction to a magnet are symptoms indicative of galvanic corrosion.

CAVITATION CORROSION

❑ Cavitation corrosion is a form of corrosive attack similar to erosion corrosion

❑ Cavitation results from collapsing bubbles created by pressure changes across metallic surfaces exposed to high velocity liquid flow.

❑ Flow across a curved surface will produce a pressure drop that would cause local boiling in the high velocity liquid if the pressure is reduced below the vapour pressure of the liquid

❑ The difference in pressure leads to the formation of gas bubbles

❑ When the bubbles land on the surface and collapse, the repeated pressure impacts are sufficient to erode or cavitate the surface

❑ The cavity formed on the surface could then turn into pits, initiating cracking

--- Page 85 ---

Cavitation is common on ship propeller, pump impellers and turbine runner subjected to high velocity liquid flow

Cavitation erosion is the major failure mechanism of many fluid machinery components

FRETTING CORROSION

❑ Fretting corrosion is a wear process enhanced by corrosion

❑ Fretting involves wear of any alloy contacting any surface in dry or humid air

❑ Fretting corrosion is a form of corrosion that, like crevice corrosion, involves corrosion where two different surfaces have an interface that brings them into quite close proximity.

❑ It is not so much electrolyte chemistry that plays an influential role. Rather, mechanical load effects such as vibration are the cause of fretting corrosion.

❑ Fretting occurs from abrasive wear of surface oxide films, which forms on contacting surfaces under load in air

--- Page 89 ---

❑ Materials with hard oxides, including stainless steels and Al and Ti alloys are particularly susceptible to this form of attack

❑ Fretting damage is characterized by Pits or grooves and oxide debris.

❑ Frettling is typically found in machinery, bolted assemblies and ball or roller bearings. Contact surfaces exposed to vibration during transportation are exposed to the risk of fretting corrosion.

Limitation of the Pourbaix diagram

❑ Pourbaix diagrams are purely based on thermodynamic data and do not provide any information on the reactions. The thermodynamic stability may not be achieved to the kinetics of the reaction. No information is provided on the rates of reaction.

❑ Consideration is given only to equilibrium conditions in specified environment and factors, such as temperature and velocity are not considered in the Pourbaix diagram. These factors are known to have significant effect on the corrosion rate.

❑ Pourbaix diagrams deal with pure metals which are not of much interest to the engineers.

Saturated Calomel electrode

❑ SCE is the most popular secondary electrode for common laboratory use.
❑ It consists of mercurous chloride HgCl2 mixed in a mercury paste on a pool of liquid mercury in contact with a saturated KCl.
❑ Electrical contact is made via an inert platinum wire immersed in mercury
❑ SCE should be stored in just slightly less than saturated KCl solution.
❑ SCE is affected by temperature and is not recommended for measurements above 50 oC
